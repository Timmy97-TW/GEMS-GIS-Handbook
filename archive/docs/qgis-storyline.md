Taiwan's shape gives the story away before a single data point is introduced. Thick in the middle, narrowing at both ends — close enough to a sweet potato that Taiwanese people have long called themselves "children of the sweet potato." During wartime shortages, when rice ran short, sweet potato was mixed in to stretch what little remained, quietly keeping people fed. Rice is the staple that built daily life on this island; sweet potato is the crop that carried people through its hardest years. These aren't two crops chosen for agronomic convenience. They are the two foods Taiwan has leaned on to survive, and they now sit on opposite ends of the same climate fault line — one exposed to salt, the other to heat.

Rice carries the salinity story, and the data leaves little room for doubt. Among the crops we evaluated, rice has the steepest salinity penalty of all — a threshold of just 3 dS/m, past which yield drops roughly 12% for every additional 1 dS/m, nearly double wheat's 7.1% penalty rate. The mechanism explains why: rice has almost no ability to keep sodium out at the root, so salt moves quickly into its tissues and, once it reaches the flowering stage, disrupts pollen formation itself — leaving a plant that looks perfectly alive while quietly producing nothing. This isn't an abstract vulnerability for Taiwan. Yunlin County, the island's single largest rice-growing region, sits on a low-lying river floodplain that has also suffered some of Taiwan's worst land subsidence from decades of groundwater overdraft — sinking parts of the county toward sea level and opening the door for seawater to intrude exactly where rice is grown. Salt doesn't have to travel far to reach the crop least equipped to keep it out.

Sweet potato carries the heat story, and it echoes rice's in an almost eerie way. Tuberization — the actual formation of the root Taiwanese families have depended on for generations — begins failing at night temperatures above 18°C and can stop almost entirely past 25°C, even while the above-ground plant keeps growing normally at day temperatures up to 32°C without visible damage. Field studies have recorded up to a 56% drop in marketable tuber ratio under heat stress, with projections showing up to 66% fewer mature tubers as warming continues. Like rice's pollen failing invisibly inside an intact-looking flower, a sweet potato plant can look completely healthy above the soil while producing almost nothing beneath it. Two different crops, two different mechanisms, the same quiet failure — damage a farmer can't see coming until harvest day.

And these crops don't just share a threat in the abstract — they share a home. Yunlin County is both Taiwan's top rice-producing region and the source of roughly 40% of the nation's sweet potato, generating NT$94.8 billion in agricultural output in 2024 alone, the highest of any county in the country — while absorbing tens of billions of NT dollars in climate-related agricultural losses every single year for the past decade. Rice and sweet potato don't just symbolize Taiwanese identity in parallel; they are grown in the same soil, tended by many of the same families, exposed to the same warming, salting climate at the same time.

This is why our biomanufacturing approach isn't speculative — it mirrors a defense these plants have already been attempting on their own. OsLEA14-A, a protein already studied in rice, has been shown to shield cellular components from both salinity and heat damage. IbLEA14, its counterpart in sweet potato, works by reinforcing cell walls against the same osmotic and ionic stress. These plants have been fighting back the whole time — quietly, and not quite well enough. Rice and sweet potato are not just Taiwan's most eaten crops. They are Taiwan's most literal expression of tradition meeting an unforgiving climate, and the clearest possible proof that the tools to help them fight back already exist. What's missing isn't the science. It's getting it to the fields fast enough to matter.

  
  

Everyone assumes rice is threatened by hot days. The real damage happens while it's asleep.

A landmark study out of the International Rice Research Institute tracked 24 years of field data and found something nobody expected: daytime maximum temperature had almost no measurable effect on rice yield. What actually predicted yield loss — with striking precision — was nighttime minimum temperature. For every 1°C increase in nighttime lows during the growing season, rice yield dropped by 10%. Not gradually. Not as one factor among many. As the dominant signal, while the scorching noon sun that everyone assumes is the villain turned out to be statistically almost irrelevant.

The mechanism is almost poetic once you understand it: during the day, rice photosynthesizes and builds up sugars. At night, it's supposed to rest — respiration slows, and the plant conserves what it built. But warmer nights keep the plant's metabolism running hot even in the dark, burning through those stored sugars before they can be converted into grain. The rice isn't dying from exposure. It's quietly starving itself in its sleep, spending on maintenance what it should have been saving for the harvest — and a farmer standing in the field at sunrise would see nothing wrong. The plant looks the same. It just has less and less to show for each night that passed.

This is your baseball moment. It has the exact same shape: an invisible physical mechanism, hiding behind the wrong intuitive explanation, quantified with startling precision, changing something people assumed they already understood. Except instead of a home run sailing an extra few feet, it's the food that over 2 billion people depend on, failing silently overnight, in fields where the farmer did everything right and still has no way to see it happening.

And it plugs directly into everything you've already built: Taiwan's nights are warming, just like everywhere else — and your own dataset gives you Taiwan-specific minimum temperature values by station and month, meaning you don't have to cite the Philippines study alone. By utilizing a bioreactor system combining both AI prediction models and stress sensors, an optogenetic system controls B. subtilis to produce and excrete our given protectant (ACCD, LEA14, BoPEP4) under different stress thresholds for abiotic and biotic stress, respectively. 

  

We have tested both trehalose and chenghan peptide/protectant on arabidopsis under both salinity and heat stress. As well, we have also tested both trehalose and chenghan peptide/ protectant on arabidopsis under both salinity and heat stress. We are planning to start testing ACCD before, during, and after salinity stress on arabidopsis to look into the efficiency; as well, we are planning on doing heat and salinity test trials on ACCD after hydroponics and agar testing is completed.

  

Our experimental molecular cloning process consists of three main components; the constitutive promoter, the inducible promoter, and the functional assay. The functional assay utilizes AmilCP, GanA-GalA, and m. Scarlet to visualize the growth curve and secretion of the protein within the bioreactor. These chromoproteins will then be replaced with our protectants with constitutive promoters, visualizing the actual growth and secretion rate of the protectant in the system. Lastly utilizing the inducible promoter modules, the circuit can be light induced and the protectant will be produced when signaled within the Bioreactor. 

  

Ultimately, when traditional expertise struggles to respond, ReLeaf puts forward a biomanufacturing resolution that, instead of replacing instincts, aims to help it move as fast as the climate now does. Through this, we hope to alleviate the struggles that farmers are facing to mitigate the effects of climate change and preserve self-sustainability in the Taiwanese food industry.
