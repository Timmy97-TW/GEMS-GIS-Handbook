4:00 PM
Taiwan, barely 400 km long, holds both a tropical climate and a subtropical one at the same zone. The journey from Kaohsiung to Yushan, which takes approximately three hours by car, traverses a temperature greater than that between Miami and Anchorage, Alaska, with a of __ °C. This variation has significance way beyond simple weather fluctuations. The presence of two highly distinct climates within just 200 kilometers of land means that it can be incredibly hard to generalize knowledge and create a cohesive narrative about what Taiwan is. This is what topographical variation means: in most countries, climate changes gradually across hundreds of kilometers. In Taiwan, it can change by double digits within the space of a single county. Aside from large regional variations in temperature that can occur across Taiwan as one travels from North to South, over the past decades, Taiwan’s average temperature has also risen significantly. To put things into perspective: over the past 3 decades, Taiwan’s climate did not just warm up, the rate at which it is changing also sped up. For nearly a century, our island’s temperature crept upward by about 0.11 degrees Celsius every ten years, a pace so slow that a farmer could spend an entire career barely noticing it. In Taiwan, flat plains can allow for rice to thrive, while tea plants thrive in high mountains only an hour away. However, since 1991, this rate has almost tripled, to 0.29 degrees Celsius per decade. Notably, 2024 became the hottest year Taiwan has ever recorded, and Taipei alone logged 63 days above 35 degrees Celsius – nearly twenty more scorching days than the historical norm. To understand how the aforementioned acceleration is felt on the ground: in an average year, Kaohsiung’s lowland rice fields sit at a scorching 29.1 degrees Celsius, while Yushan’s alpine terraces (barely 200 km away) hover near a wintry 10.2, dipping below freezing in January.
4:00 PM
The acceleration of climate change in Taiwan in parallel to the dire regional variations is exactly why Taiwanese agricultural knowledge was never uniform. Instead, it was built upon thousands of hyper-specific inherited bits of wisdom. In a situation where rising temperature is combined with, for example, an increase in soil salinity, small-scale farmers across Taiwan often do not have the luxury to exchange farming expertise with one another. The people most affected by climate change are also usually the oldest. The average Taiwanese farmer is 63.5 years old, and more than half of all farmers are over 65. This isn’t a workforce with decades left to retrain, relocate, and rebuild after a disaster. Among these communities, alpine farmers are the most vulnerable. As global warming increases the average temperature on low-land, cooler-climate plants move further up the mountain, slowly encroaching on other plants. This subtle encroachment means alpine farmers no longer have any higher, cooler ground left to retreat to anymore. In the face of shifting weather patterns, farmers have persevered so far with centuries of experience and wisdom. However, in the past decades, climate unpredictability has led to a gap between how fast tradition can adapt and how fast the climate is now moving. This is exactly where biomanufacturing belongs: not replacing what farmers know, but giving them a modern tool built to move at the same speed as the problem.
